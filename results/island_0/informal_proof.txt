1. Let \(m_0,n_0\) be distinct positive integers. Define sequences \((m_k),(n_k)\) of positive integers such that for each \(k\ge 0\),
   \[
   \frac{m_{k+1}}{n_{k+1}}=\frac{2m_k+1}{2n_k+1},
   \]
   and (for \(k+1>0\), i.e. for all \(k\ge 0\)) the integers \(m_{k+1}\) and \(n_{k+1}\) are relatively prime.

2. For each \(k\ge 0\), define
   \[
   A_k := 2m_k+1,\qquad B_k := 2n_k+1.
   \]

3. Note that \(A_k\) and \(B_k\) are positive odd integers for every \(k\), since \(2m_k\) and \(2n_k\) are even.

4. For each \(k\ge 0\), define
   \[
   d_k := \gcd(A_k,B_k)=\gcd(2m_k+1,\,2n_k+1).
   \]

5. Our goal is to show that \(d_k=1\) for all but finitely many \(k\), i.e. \(\{k : d_k>1\}\) is finite.

---

### Step 1: “Reducing the fraction” identifies \(m_{k+1},n_{k+1}\)

6. Fix \(k\ge 0\). By definition of \(d_k\), we can write
   \[
   A_k = d_k a_k,\qquad B_k = d_k b_k,
   \]
   where \(a_k,b_k\) are positive integers and \(\gcd(a_k,b_k)=1\). (This is the standard property of the gcd: dividing both numbers by their gcd yields coprime quotients.)

7. Then
   \[
   \frac{A_k}{B_k}=\frac{d_k a_k}{d_k b_k}=\frac{a_k}{b_k}.
   \]

8. By the recurrence hypothesis,
   \[
   \frac{m_{k+1}}{n_{k+1}} = \frac{A_k}{B_k} = \frac{a_k}{b_k}.
   \]

9. We also know \(\gcd(m_{k+1},n_{k+1})=1\) (since \(k+1>0\)) and \(\gcd(a_k,b_k)=1\).

10. A basic uniqueness fact about reduced fractions is: if \(\frac{x}{y}=\frac{u}{v}\) with \(x,y,u,v\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}\) and \(\gcd(x,y)=\gcd(u,v)=1\), then \(x=u\) and \(y=v\). (Proof: from \(xv=yu\), use \(\gcd(x,y)=1\) to show \(x\mid u\) and similarly \(u\mid x\), hence \(x=u\), and likewise \(y=v\).)

11. Applying this uniqueness to step 8, we conclude
   \[
   m_{k+1}=a_k=\frac{A_k}{d_k}=\frac{2m_k+1}{d_k},\qquad
   n_{k+1}=b_k=\frac{B_k}{d_k}=\frac{2n_k+1}{d_k}.
   \]

12. In particular, \(d_k\mid (2m_k+1)\) and \(d_k\mid (2n_k+1)\), and dividing by \(d_k\) produces the next coprime pair.

---

### Step 2: Track the difference \(m_k-n_k\)

13. Define the (nonzero) integer difference
   \[
   \Delta_k := m_k - n_k.
   \]

14. First we prove \(\Delta_k\neq 0\) for all \(k\ge 0\).

15. Base case: \(\Delta_0=m_0-n_0\neq 0\) since \(m_0\neq n_0\) is given.

16. Inductive step: assume \(\Delta_k\neq 0\). If \(\Delta_{k+1}=0\), then \(m_{k+1}=n_{k+1}\), so
   \[
   \frac{m_{k+1}}{n_{k+1}}=1.
   \]
   By the recurrence,
   \[
   1=\frac{m_{k+1}}{n_{k+1}}=\frac{2m_k+1}{2n_k+1}.
   \]
   Cross-multiplying gives \(2m_k+1=2n_k+1\), hence \(m_k=n_k\), i.e. \(\Delta_k=0\), contradicting the inductive hypothesis. Thus \(\Delta_{k+1}\neq 0\).

17. By induction, \(\Delta_k\neq 0\) for all \(k\).

18. Now compute \(\Delta_{k+1}\) in terms of \(\Delta_k\), using step 11:
   \[
   \Delta_{k+1}
   = m_{k+1}-n_{k+1}
   = \frac{2m_k+1}{d_k}-\frac{2n_k+1}{d_k}
   = \frac{(2m_k+1)-(2n_k+1)}{d_k}
   = \frac{2(m_k-n_k)}{d_k}
   = \frac{2\Delta_k}{d_k}.
   \]
   So
   \[
   \Delta_{k+1}=\frac{2\Delta_k}{d_k}. \tag{\(*\)}
   \]

---

### Step 3: Show \(d_k\) divides \(\Delta_k\) whenever \(d_k>1\) (in fact always)

19. Recall \(d_k=\gcd(2m_k+1,2n_k+1)\). Therefore \(d_k\mid (2m_k+1)\) and \(d_k\mid (2n_k+1)\).

20. Subtracting the two divisible quantities, we get
   \[
   d_k \mid \big((2m_k+1)-(2n_k+1)\big)=2(m_k-n_k)=2\Delta_k.
   \]

21. From step 3, \(2m_k+1\) and \(2n_k+1\) are odd, so their gcd \(d_k\) is also odd.

22. Since \(d_k\) is odd, \(\gcd(d_k,2)=1\).

23. A standard divisibility fact: if \(\gcd(d,2)=1\) and \(d\mid 2X\), then \(d\mid X\). (Proof: because \(d\) divides \(2X\) and is coprime to \(2\), the factor \(2\) is invertible mod \(d\), so \(X\equiv 0\pmod d\).)

24. Apply this with \(d=d_k\) and \(X=\Delta_k\): from step 20 and step 22, conclude
   \[
   d_k \mid \Delta_k. \tag{\(**\)}
   \]

25. In particular, if \(d_k>1\), then \(d_k\) is an odd integer \(\ge 3\) dividing \(\Delta_k\).

---

### Step 4: Introduce the odd part of \(|\Delta_k|\) and show it strictly decreases when \(d_k>1\)

26. For any nonzero integer \(x\), write \(|x| = 2^{e(x)}\cdot \operatorname{odd}(x)\), where \(e(x)\ge 0\) and \(\operatorname{odd}(x)\) is a positive odd integer. (This is the unique factorization of \(|x|\) into a power of \(2\) times an odd number; \(\operatorname{odd}(x)\) is often called the “odd part” of \(|x|\).)

27. For each \(k\), define
   \[
   t_k := \operatorname{odd}(\Delta_k),
   \]
   i.e. \(t_k\) is the odd part of \(|\Delta_k|\). By step 17, \(\Delta_k\neq 0\), so \(t_k\) is well-defined and \(t_k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 1}\) is odd.

28. We will show:  
   - If \(d_k=1\), then \(t_{k+1}=t_k\).  
   - If \(d_k>1\), then \(t_{k+1}=t_k/d_k < t_k\).  
   In particular, \(t_k\) strictly decreases whenever \(d_k>1\).

29. Start from the exact recurrence \((*)\):
   \[
   \Delta_{k+1}=\frac{2\Delta_k}{d_k}.
   \]

30. Take absolute values:
   \[
   |\Delta_{k+1}| = \left|\frac{2\Delta_k}{d_k}\right| = \frac{2|\Delta_k|}{d_k}.
   \]

31. Write \(|\Delta_k|=2^{e_k}t_k\) with \(t_k\) odd (by definition of \(t_k\)).

32. Substitute into step 30:
   \[
   |\Delta_{k+1}| = \frac{2\cdot 2^{e_k}t_k}{d_k} = 2^{e_k+1}\cdot \frac{t_k}{d_k}.
   \]

33. By \((**)\), \(d_k\mid \Delta_k\). Since \(d_k\) is odd (step 21), \(d_k\) must divide the odd part \(t_k\) (because an odd divisor cannot “hide” inside a power of \(2\)).

34. Therefore \(\frac{t_k}{d_k}\) is an integer, and moreover it is odd because it is (odd)/(odd).

35. Now step 32 expresses \(|\Delta_{k+1}|\) as a power of \(2\) (namely \(2^{e_k+1}\)) times the odd integer \(\frac{t_k}{d_k}\).

36. By uniqueness of the decomposition into \(2\)-power times an odd number, the odd part of \(|\Delta_{k+1}|\) is exactly \(\frac{t_k}{d_k}\). In other words,
   \[
   t_{k+1}=\frac{t_k}{d_k}. \tag{\(\dagger\)}
   \]

37. If \(d_k=1\), then \((\dagger)\) gives \(t_{k+1}=t_k\).

38. If \(d_k>1\), then \(d_k\ge 3\) (step 25), so \((\dagger)\) gives
   \[
   t_{k+1}=\frac{t_k}{d_k}\le \frac{t_k}{3} < t_k.
   \]
   Thus \(t_{k+1}<t_k\) whenever \(d_k>1\).

---

### Step 5: Conclude only finitely many \(k\) have \(d_k>1\)

39. The sequence \((t_k)_{k\ge 0}\) consists of positive integers (step 27).

40. Step 38 shows that every time \(d_k>1\), the integer \(t_k\) strictly decreases.

41. A strictly decreasing sequence of positive integers cannot decrease infinitely many times, because it would eventually fall below \(1\), which is impossible.

42. Therefore there are only finitely many indices \(k\) such that \(d_k>1\).

43. By definition of \(d_k\) (step 4), the condition \(d_k>1\) is exactly the condition that \(2m_k+1\) and \(2n_k+1\) are **not** relatively prime.

44. Hence the set
   \[
   \{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0} : \gcd(2m_k+1,2n_k+1)>1\}
   \]
   is finite.

45. Equivalently, \(2m_k+1\) and \(2n_k+1\) are relatively prime for all but finitely many positive integers \(k\), which is exactly what we needed to prove.
